# Policy Note: Bangladesh's Exchange-Rate Reform and External Vulnerability — An Early Assessment

*Based solely on the finalized findings in `paper/final_paper.md`, `RESEARCH_LEDGER.md`, and `STRESS_TEST.md`. Descriptive and mechanical findings only.*

## Key Message

Bangladesh's observed exchange-market pressure was descriptively lower, on average, in the twelve months after its mid-May 2025 move to a more flexible exchange-rate arrangement (a crawling peg with band) than in the twelve months before. However, Pakistan and Vietnam show the same directional pattern over the identical period, so this cannot be read as evidence that the reform itself caused the improvement. A stylized four-quarter stress test indicates that plausible remittance, export, and import-price shocks would mechanically imply external-account deterioration on the order of $12 billion for Bangladesh.

## What Changed

Bangladesh introduced a crawling-peg exchange-rate mechanism in May 2024, followed by a further move to a more flexible arrangement — a crawling peg with band — in mid-May 2025, under its IMF-supported financing program. The exchange rate has continued to move in discrete steps continuously, consistent with the documented crawling-peg-with-band arrangement.

## What the Data Show

Using a standard, published Exchange Market Pressure index (Kaminsky, Lizondo, and Reinhart, 1998) built from verified IMF reserve and exchange-rate data:

- Bangladesh's mean EMP fell from **0.0072** (12 months pre-reform) to **−0.0060** (12 months post-reform), with volatility little changed.
- The May 2025 reform month itself was not unusually large relative to the preceding 12-month empirical distribution.
- Post-reform, most of the movement in the pressure index came from a substantial rise in reserves including a single large increase in June 2025. Exchange-rate movements were small and stable after the reform.
- **The same pre-to-post decline in average pressure also appears in Pakistan and Vietnam over the identical calendar window**, neither of which had a comparable reform. This is the central caveat on the finding above: in this descriptive design, Bangladesh's pattern cannot be disentangled from common external conditions (for example, global energy prices, which spiked within the post-reform window).

## Stress-Test Takeaway

A transparent, mechanical scenario applies a 10 percent decline in remittances, a 5 percent decline in exports, and a global energy-price shock translated into a stylized 10 percent rise in import costs, to the latest four-quarter observed external accounts:

| Country | Combined external-account deterioration |
|---|---|
| Bangladesh | approximately −$12.3 billion |
| Pakistan | approximately −$11.9 billion |
| Vietnam (export and import-price shocks only; remittance data unresolved) | approximately −$70.6 billion |

These figures show the mechanically implied cumulative external-account deterioration these specific, illustrative shocks would produce. Actual outcomes would depend on financing flows, exchange-rate adjustment, capital flows, and policy response, none of which are modeled here.

## Main Vulnerabilities / Policy Implications

- **Bangladesh's current account remains thin** (a baseline of roughly $161 million over the latest observed four quarters), leaving limited buffer against combined external shocks of the scale modeled here — a mechanical illustration of financing pressure.
- **Reserve accumulation observed since the reform should not be read as confirmation that the new exchange-rate arrangement has durably reduced external pressure**, given that the same directional pattern appears in comparator countries over the same period.
- **Monitoring should distinguish the exchange-rate channel from the reserves channel of pressure** — in Bangladesh's post-reform data, reserves have driven most of the observed change in pressure, not the exchange rate itself, which has moved in discrete, managed steps.
- **Global energy-price conditions are a relevant external risk factor to monitor alongside the reform's progress**, given the documented price spike within the post-reform window and Bangladesh's exposure to import-price shocks under the stress test.

## Important Caveat on Causality

**None of the findings above establish that the May 2025 reform caused any observed change in external pressure.** The comparator evidence from Pakistan and Vietnam is a first-order reason for caution: absent a research design capable of separating the reform's effect from common external conditions, the appropriate reading of the Bangladesh evidence is descriptive and associative: "coincided with," not "was caused by." The stress-test results are illustrative mechanical scenarios, not forecasts of future reserves, the exchange rate, or GDP.
