# Exchange-Rate Flexibility and External Adjustment: An Early Comparative Assessment of Bangladesh, Pakistan, and Vietnam

## Abstract

In May 2025, Bangladesh Bank introduced a more flexible exchange-rate arrangement (a crawling peg with band), following an exchange-rate adjustment in May 2024. This note uses a two-component Exchange Market Pressure (EMP) index (Kaminsky, Lizondo, and Reinhart, 1998) built from verified IMF reserve and exchange-rate data to describe how Bangladesh's external-pressure dynamics evolved around the reform, benchmarked against Pakistan and Vietnam over the identical calendar window. Bangladesh's mean EMP fell from 0.0072 in the twelve months before the reform to −0.0060 in the twelve months after, with volatility broadly unchanged. However, Pakistan and Vietnam show the same directional decline over the identical period, which prevents attributing Bangladesh's pattern to the reform itself. A stylized four-quarter external-shock stress test combined a 10 percent remittance decline, a 5 percent export decline, and a 10 percent import-value increase mechanically linked to a global energy-price shock. It implies a combined external-account deterioration of approximately $12.3 billion for Bangladesh and $11.9 billion for Pakistan, and $70.6 billion for Vietnam on a narrower two-shock basis (Vietnam's national monthly remittance data remain unresolved). These results are descriptive and mechanical, not causal estimates or forecasts.

## 1. Introduction

Bangladesh moved toward a more flexible exchange-rate arrangement in mid-May 2025 — specifically, a crawling peg with band. An earlier exchange-rate adjustment took place in May 2024 under its IMF-supported program. This is not characterized here as a free float or fully flexible exchange rate; both the underlying IMF documentation and the observed data (Section 4) describe and show a managed, step-like adjustment process rather than continuous market-determined pricing.

This note asks two questions. First, how did Bangladesh's external-sector pressure evolve around the May 2025 reform, relative to its own pre-reform history and relative to Pakistan and Vietnam over the same calendar period? Second, what does a simple, transparent stress test suggest about the scale of external-account vulnerability that would mechanically follow from plausible shocks to remittances, exports, and import prices?

Both questions are answered descriptively. This note does not estimate the causal effect of the reform, and it does not forecast reserves, the exchange rate, or GDP. It reports what the verified data show under a fixed, pre-specified analytical design, and it is explicit about the limits of that design — most importantly, that Pakistan and Vietnam exhibit the same directional shift in mean EMP over the identical window, which is a first-order reason not to read Bangladesh's pattern as reform-specific.

## 2. Institutional Context: The May 2024 and May 2025 Exchange-Rate Changes

Bangladesh Bank adjusted the exchange rate in May 2024, as an interim step under Bangladesh's IMF-supported financing arrangement. In mid-May 2025, Bangladesh Bank introduced a more flexible exchange-rate arrangement — described in the IMF's 2025 Article IV Consultation staff report as "the new crawling peg with the band regime." This is the first crawling-peg-with-band framework the available primary source describes; it is not characterized here as a continuation or replacement of an earlier crawling-peg regime, since the same source refers to the May 2024 change only as "the exchange rate adjustment." This characterization is used throughout this note; the reform is not described as a free float, and no primary source available to this project's research process describes it as such.

The two-step nature of the transition (the May 2024 adjustment, then the May 2025 reform) matters for interpreting the data: as shown in Section 6, the single largest EMP observation in Bangladesh's entire 2018–2026 sample occurs in May 2024, near the earlier exchange-rate adjustment, not at the May 2025 reform date itself.

## 3. Data and Methodology

**Data.** All series used in this note were sourced directly from primary IMF datasets (International Liquidity for reserves excluding gold; Exchange Rates for the nominal exchange rate; Balance of Payments for the current account and goods trade; World Economic Outlook for GDP growth and CPI inflation, not used as reform-window evidence; and the Primary Commodity Price System for the global energy-price proxy), plus Bangladesh Bank and State Bank of Pakistan national remittance releases. Every series was independently inspected before use: coverage, missing observations, sign conventions, and definitional caveats. This verification record is maintained in the project's research ledger and is not repeated here in full. One data limitation carries through the whole note: Vietnam's nationwide monthly remittance series is unresolved (no suitable primary source was located), so Vietnam is excluded from any monthly remittance comparison and from the remittance-shock component of the stress test.

**EMP methodology.** This note uses the two-component Exchange Market Pressure index of Kaminsky, Lizondo, and Reinhart (1998, *IMF Staff Papers*):

EMP<sub>i,t</sub> = %Δe<sub>i,t</sub> − α<sub>i</sub>·%Δr<sub>i,t</sub>

where e is the nominal exchange rate (local currency per US dollar, end-of-period) and r is international reserves excluding gold. The weight α<sub>i</sub> is computed separately for each country as the ratio of the standard deviation of exchange-rate changes to the standard deviation of reserve changes, over the full common sample (2018-M01 to 2026-M06), so that reserve changes (typically far more volatile) do not mechanically dominate the index. A rise in EMP indicates depreciation pressure, reserve loss, or both; a fall indicates the opposite. This index was selected because it is directly implementable with the two variables this project retained (reserves and the exchange rate), it does not require policy-rate data (which this project's scope excludes), and it is one of the most widely used specifications in the empirical currency-pressure literature, published directly by the IMF. KLR's original mean-plus-three-standard-deviation crisis-dating threshold is deliberately **not** applied in this note; the continuous EMP series is used descriptively, and no month is labeled a "crisis."

**What EMP does and does not tell us.** EMP is a descriptive measure of observed exchange-rate and reserve pressure. It does not decompose that pressure into causes, and it does not identify the effect of any specific policy action, including the May 2025 reform. A change in EMP around a reform date is a coincidence worth investigating further, and this note treats it accordingly throughout.

## 4. Descriptive Evidence Around May 2025

Bangladesh's EMP over the full sample is shown in **Figure 1**. Using a fixed 12-month pre-reform window (May 2024–April 2025), the May 2025 reform month itself, and a fixed 12-month post-reform window (June 2025–May 2026):

- Pre-reform mean EMP: **0.0072**
- May 2025 EMP: **0.0190**
- Post-reform mean EMP: **−0.0060**
- Full historical mean EMP (2018-M02–2026-M06): **0.0037**

Post-reform EMP volatility was broadly similar to the historical level (post-reform standard deviation 0.0175 versus a full-historical standard deviation of 0.0160). May 2025 itself, at 0.41 standard deviations above the pre-reform mean, is not unusually large relative to the preceding 12-month empirical distribution.

Two further facts are worth stating precisely, because of their timing rather than any claim about cause: the largest positive EMP observation in Bangladesh's entire sample is **May 2024 (+0.0814)** — coinciding with the May 2024 exchange-rate adjustment, not the May 2025 reform — and the smallest is **June 2025 (−0.0539)**, the month immediately following the May 2025 reform. Both are reported descriptively as coincidences in timing.

**Figure 2** decomposes the post-reform period into its two EMP components. Post-reform exchange-rate changes were small relative to reserve movements: the mean monthly exchange-rate change post-reform was close to zero (mean −0.01 percent, standard deviation 0.30 percent), while reserves rose substantially and unevenly over the same window (mean monthly change +3.64 percent, standard deviation 10.39 percent), including a single large increase of +32.75 percent in June 2025 (from approximately $19.0 billion to $25.2 billion). Descriptively, most of the post-reform movement in EMP is reflects the reserve component of the index rather than the exchange-rate component. The exchange rate itself moved in discrete steps (approximately 117.7 to 122.75 BDT/USD across the full window, essentially flat for the twelve months after the reform) — a pattern consistent with the documented crawling-peg-with-band arrangement. No counterfactual market-clearing exchange rate is inferred from this pattern.

## 5. Cross-Country Comparison

**Figure 3** and **Table 3** show the same EMP construction for Pakistan and Vietnam over the identical calendar windows.

| Country | Pre-reform mean (SD) | May 2025 | Post-reform mean (SD) | Full historical mean (SD) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bangladesh | 0.0072 (0.0284) | 0.0190 | −0.0060 (0.0175) | 0.0037 (0.0160) |
| Pakistan | −0.0023 (0.0159) | −0.0288 | −0.0113 (0.0205) | 0.0069 (0.0597) |
| Vietnam | 0.0035 (0.0053) | 0.0015 | 0.0000 (0.0039) | 0.0004 (0.0052) |

This comparison is the central limitation of the descriptive findings above. **All three countries show a decline in mean EMP from the pre-reform to the post-reform window**. Pakistan and Vietnam had no comparable exchange-rate reform during this period. In this descriptive design, Bangladesh's pattern cannot be disentangled from common external conditions that may have affected all three countries over the same window; **Figure 4** shows that a global energy-price spike occurred in March 2026, inside the post-reform window for all three countries, as one example of such a common condition. This note does not attempt to isolate how much of Bangladesh's shift, if any, is attributable to the reform versus common conditions — doing so would require a causal research design (e.g., a difference-in-differences approach with a defensible comparison group) that is explicitly outside this note's scope. Pakistan's EMP is also an order of magnitude more volatile than Bangladesh's or Vietnam's across the full sample (full-historical standard deviation 0.0597 versus 0.0160 and 0.0052 respectively); Figure 3 presents each country on its own y-axis scale for this reason.

## 6. Four-Quarter Stylized Stress Test

To assess remaining external vulnerability under plausible shock scenarios, this note applies a mechanical, transparent stress test to the latest available four-quarter observed baseline (2025-Q2 to 2026-Q1) for each country. This is **not a forecast**: it applies fixed, pre-specified percentage shocks to observed historical flows and reports the resulting arithmetic, with no modeled financing response, exchange-rate adjustment, or policy reaction.

**Shocks applied:** remittances −10 percent (Bangladesh and Pakistan only — Vietnam's national monthly remittance series remains unresolved and was not fabricated or substituted); goods exports −5 percent (all three countries); and a global energy-price shock of +10 percent, mechanically translated into a stylized +10 percent increase in the value of goods imports under the assumption that real import quantities are held constant. This last assumption is a simplifying scenario choice and the underlying energy-price series is a global proxy.

**Table 4** summarizes the combined-scenario results:

| Country | Baseline current account ($mn) | Combined external-account deterioration ($mn) | Mechanically shocked current account ($mn) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bangladesh | 161.0 | −12,258.1 | −12,097.1 |
| Pakistan | 450.0 | −11,850.3 | −11,400.4 |
| Vietnam (export + import-price shocks only) | 31,023.0 | −70,616.2 | −39,593.2 |

Vietnam's figure is not directly comparable to Bangladesh's and Pakistan's, since it reflects only two of the three shocks; this is stated explicitly wherever Vietnam's stress-test result appears.

**What this means.** The deterioration figures represent the cumulative external-account deterioration that would mechanically follow from these specific illustrative shocks over four quarters, holding everything else constant. They are not reserve forecasts. Whether or how such a deterioration would actually affect reserves depends on financing flows, exchange-rate adjustment, capital account movements, valuation effects, and policy response — none of which are modeled here. No claim is made that reserves would fall by any specific amount, or at all.

## 7. Limitations

- **This is a descriptive, fixed-window comparison, not a causal estimate.** No statistical test establishes that the pre/post EMP difference for Bangladesh is distinguishable from ordinary variation, and no comparison-group-adjusted design was applied.
- **Pakistan and Vietnam show the same directional EMP shift over the identical calendar window as Bangladesh.** This is the single most important limitation on any reading of the Bangladesh results as reform-specific.
- **A global energy-price spike falls inside the post-reform window**, a plausible confounding external condition not incorporated into the EMP calculation itself.
- **Vietnam's monthly national remittances remain unresolved.** No three-country monthly remittance comparison was attempted, and Vietnam's stress-test combined figure uses only two of three shocks.
- **GDP growth and CPI inflation were not used as reform-window evidence**, given Bangladesh's fiscal-year reporting convention (versus Vietnam's calendar-year convention) and an explicit actual-versus-projection caveat on 2025–2026 WEO figures.
- **The stress test's shock magnitudes and import-price transmission assumption are imposed, illustrative choices, not estimated parameters.** The same energy-price shock was applied uniformly to all three countries despite differing actual energy-import exposure.
- **External debt, the real effective exchange rate, and policy interest rates were excluded from this project's scope by design**, and are not available as supporting evidence anywhere in this note.
- **KLR's original reserve concept (IFS line "1L.d") and this project's IMF International Liquidity "reserves excluding gold" series are conceptually aligned on strong convergent evidence, not a single confirmed primary citation.**

## 8. Conclusion

Bangladesh's external-pressure dynamics, as measured by a standard two-component EMP index, were descriptively lower on average in the twelve months following its mid-May 2025 move to a more flexible exchange-rate arrangement (a crawling peg with band) than in the twelve months before. The exchange-rate reform month itself was not unusually large relative to the preceding 12-month empirical distribution. This pattern coincided with a substantial rise in reserves, particularly in June 2025, and post-reform EMP movement is attributable mostly to the reserve component of the index. However, Pakistan and Vietnam show the same directional decline in mean EMP over the identical window, so in this descriptive design, Bangladesh's pattern cannot be disentangled from common external conditions, including a documented energy-price spike within the post-reform window. A four-quarter stylized stress test suggests mechanically implied external-account deterioration on the order of $12 billion for Bangladesh and Pakistan and $70 billion for Vietnam (on a narrower basis) under plausible remittance, export, and import-price shocks — a mechanical illustration. Future work extending the post-reform window, incorporating a defensible comparison-group design, and resolving Vietnam's remittance data gap would be needed before any causal or predictive claim could be supported.
